    Zotero¶

    Zotero is a free reference manager — the citation half of writing a paper, and (with one extra plug-in) the thing that feeds Claude's literature workflow without you naming files by hand. This page covers what it is, when it earns its place, what the storage tiers actually limit, and how it plugs into the kit. It is optional throughout; the second section says when you can skip it entirely.

    What it is, in one line¶

    Zotero = a free desktop app where you save every paper you've read, with one click from your browser, and out comes a .bib file your LaTeX paper cites against.

    Do you even need Zotero? (sometimes no)¶

    Worth saying up front: Zotero is optional. Everything in the kit works without it. You can manage citations entirely by hand — drop a PDF into your project's literature/<citekey>.pdf folder, add a matching entry to references.bib, and \citep{...} it from the paper. That is a valid workflow and some economists run it for years.

    What Zotero buys you is the automation around that workflow:

    • One click from a journal page (or Google Scholar) saves both the metadata and the PDF.
    • Browser-side citekey generation (via Better BibTeX) means your authorYEARkeyword citekeys are produced for you, consistently.
    • /sync-pdfs pulls every PDF that's been added to a Zotero collection into your project's literature/ folder, named by citekey — no manual rename step.
    • /sync-bib keeps your project's references.bib in sync with Zotero, so a citation you just added in the browser is available in paper.tex minutes later.

    If you read 100+ papers a year and your bibliography spans projects, this saves a lot of friction. If you write one paper at a time and only cite a dozen things, hand-managing the bib is fine and faster to start. I recommend Zotero once your literature collection outgrows your memory of what's in it.

    Install + Zotero Connector (the browser plug-in)¶

    Install Zotero from https://www.zotero.org/download/; pick your OS, run the installer. Then install the Zotero Connector browser extension (same download page, lower half) — Firefox, Chrome, Safari, and Edge are all supported.

    The Connector is the one bit that earns the workflow. With it: open any journal article page, NBER working paper, Google Scholar result, or arXiv preprint, click the Connector button in the browser bar, and Zotero saves the metadata + PDF (where available) to your library in seconds. No manual title/author entry, no PDF download dance.

    Storage tiers — what your account level changes¶

    Zotero accounts are free, but only the metadata sync is unlimited. PDF / attachment sync is metered, and the storage plans decide how much of your library actually rides along on Zotero's servers:

    Plan Annual cost Storage
    Free $0 300 MB
    Lab $20 2 GB
    Professional $60 6 GB
    Unlimited $120 Unlimited

    For context, a typical econ PDF runs 1.5–3 MB, so 300 MB is roughly 100–200 papers depending on figure density, and 2 GB is a few thousand. Above that you're into "I read everything in my field" territory.

    What happens when you hit your limit: Zotero stops syncing new PDFs to the cloud. The metadata (title, authors, abstract, citekey) still syncs — so other devices still see the entries — but the PDF lives only on the device where you imported it. For our workflow that means /sync-pdfs running on a different machine won't see the PDF unless you upgrade or fall back to a linked-files setup (you keep the PDFs in a Dropbox/iCloud folder yourself and Zotero just stores the link).

    I run Lab tier. Free is fine if you read a modest amount, or if you keep the heavy PDFs in linked-file storage.

    Notes and annotations inside Zotero¶

    A side benefit worth flagging: Zotero is also a place to read and annotate the papers it stores. The built-in PDF reader lets you highlight, add sticky-note comments, and attach a free-form note to each paper entry. Those notes sync across your devices and stay attached to the paper forever — when you cite it three years later, your highlights are still there.

    This is genuinely useful and underused. A two-line note ("this is the paper that shows the IV is invalid when X holds — see footnote 7") on every paper you read pays for itself the first time you go looking for it during a referee response.

    How Zotero fits our workflow¶

    This is the part specific to the kit. Three skills bridge Zotero into the project:

    • /sync-pdfs copies the PDFs from your local Zotero storage into <project>/literature/<citekey>.pdf for every citekey in the project's .bib. One-way, Zotero → project. After it runs, your literature/ folder mirrors the citations the paper actually uses.
    • /sync-bib refreshes the ZOTERO zone of references.bib from your declared Zotero libraries (group library for shared projects, your personal library for solo work) and detects orphan citations in the .tex. Two-way enough that a citekey added in Zotero in the morning is in paper.tex by lunch.
    • /lit-corpus (independent of Zotero, but pairs with it) converts the PDFs in literature/ into a markdown wiki Claude can scan token-cheaply — see literature for the rest of that pipeline.

    The citekey convention the team uses is authorYEARkeyword (acemoglu2001colonial, gehring2025narrative) and Better BibTeX generates it from a template. Install the Better BibTeX plug-in once, configure the citekey template, and every new paper you add to Zotero gets a sensible citekey automatically. Without Better BibTeX, Zotero gives you ugly numeric keys (acemoglu_colonial_2001) that you would not want in a \citep{...} call.

    When Zotero is the wrong tool¶

    A few uses people try and shouldn't:

    • A general PDF library for slides, course notes, internal reports. Zotero is for citable references — papers, books, working papers, datasets. Put internal reports somewhere else (a Dropbox folder, your project's project_management/).
    • A reading-list manager for "want to read" items separate from the references you actually cite. The collections you build inside Zotero are best aligned with projects/papers, not "to-read" piles. Keep the reading queue elsewhere.
    • A way around paywalls. Zotero respects the publishers' files: if the journal didn't ship the PDF as an open-access file, the Connector saves the metadata but not the PDF. Getting the full text is a separate problem — go through your library's subscriptions or interlibrary loan.
