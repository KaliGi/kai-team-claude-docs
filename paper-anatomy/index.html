    The anatomy of an empirical paper — the arc and the hidden decisions¶

    This page is tool-agnostic: what an empirical paper is made of, and where one quietly falls apart, no AI and no special software assumed. Why structure matters (even solo) covered where your files go; this covers what the work inside them is.

    Carry one paper through the page. Does a new paved road change what farmers grow? 480 villages, 2009–2019, roads finished in different years, so villages connected in 2012 are a comparison for those connected in 2017. Your estimate: a 0.14 rise in the log cash-crop share, standard error 0.05.

    Six months in, a discussant at a seminar asks about one of the choices behind your standard errors. You had a reason. It is nowhere: not the paper, not a code comment, not your notes.

    Nothing broke. No file was lost, no data corrupted, no step done sloppily. What was lost was a decision, and decisions are the part of a research project with no natural home. The road paper is invented; the failures in it are not.

    The arc¶

    The arc is the phases every empirical paper goes through: question → theory → data → analysis → exhibits → argument → writing → review → submission. Not a one-way street; you will revisit earlier phases, and that is normal. Naming them lets us point at one precisely, and gives each its own definition of "done" — many stalled projects are stalled because someone is using the definition from a different phase.

    Read it top to bottom. Each box is one phase, numbered in the order you first reach it; solid arrows are the forward path. The two boxed groups split the arc: the first five establish what is true, judged by whether the finding survives; the last four make a reader believe it, judged by whether an unsympathetic reader is convinced. The one solid arrow crossing between the groups, exhibits to argument, is the handover, where you stop asking what the data say and start asking what a reader will accept.

    Six dotted arrows are the returns, each labelled with its trigger. Three of the six start at analysis, which is the honest picture of where the back-and-forth concentrates: back to the question when the data can't answer it, back to data when cleaning turns up problems or a variable you need isn't there, and back to theory when the result needs a mechanism. The other three come from further along: argument back to exhibits when the story needs one that doesn't exist, review back to analysis when a check is missing, and submission back there when the formal revise-and-resubmit finally lands, years later. All six land on a phase you thought was finished, which is the most useful thing the picture says. No dotted arrow marks a failure; they are the normal shape of the work.

    What "done" means, phase by phase, on the road paper. I'd defend the first five hard, theory least of all: how much a paper needs is genuinely contested. Treat the rest as the defaults I use.

    • Question — one sentence naming the outcome, the comparison that identifies the effect, and why the answer isn't known: does road access shift crop mix toward cash crops, villages connected early against those connected later?
    • Theory — how much you need varies enormously in economics, from a framing paragraph naming the mechanism to a formal model. Done when it is precise enough to say which results would surprise you: if market access is what the road changes, villages already near a market should gain least.
    • Data — every variable in a file your code built from raw data, every source nameable, down to the merge key you check by hand on 20 villages before trusting it on 480 (the standard data provenance sets).
    • Analysis — specification fixed and the choices behind it written down: clustering level, sample window, which years drop. Then name the result that would make you drop the claim and check you ran that test. If you can't name one, the analysis isn't finished, it's just extensive.
    • Exhibits — an exhibit is any figure or table that carries evidence in the paper, the umbrella word so we don't say "figures and tables" forty times. Done when each shows one thing you can state in a sentence.
    • Argument — claims and exhibits map both ways: every claim points to evidence, every exhibit supports a claim. An exhibit supporting nothing is a missing paragraph or a table to cut, and you have to say which.
    • Writing — each paragraph delivers one takeaway you could state without rereading it. Hold on to this one; it gets a name below.
    • Review — someone whose job was to find problems has tried, and you answered each objection in writing. That runs from early on and never stops: co-author comments, the seminar discussant, the conference audience. Referee reports and the revise-and-resubmit are the same engine in formal dress, in economics typically two to three years from first submission. Your own reread counts for little: you know what you meant, so you read what you meant.
    • Submission — a stranger rebuilds every number from your raw data and code without asking you, the standard a replication package is held to and the argument for one-click replication from day one.

    The hidden decisions¶

    Each phase produces two things: the work, and the decisions about the work. A research decision is a choice you make once and then rely on many times: what to call a concept, which exhibits exist and what each shows, what a given paragraph should say, where a piece of evidence gets cited. Not the work itself, the standing choice the work then follows, which is why losing one hurts more than losing an afternoon of code.

    Hidden decisions are the research decisions everyone makes but rarely writes down. Four kinds show up in every empirical paper:

    • What each concept is called — one name per idea, plus the alternatives ruled out. That agreed name has a standard label, the canonical term, and its own page: consistent terminology.
    • Which exhibits exist and what each is named — not the numbers printed in the draft, an actual list: this figure exists, it shows this, its identifier is this.
    • What each paragraph should say — the takeaway from the writing phase above has a name: the lead, the one-sentence takeaway a paragraph is written to deliver. Decide the lead before you draft; the paragraph then earns it.
    • Where each exhibit gets cited — which section, which paragraph, in what role. An exhibit cited nowhere is invisible; one cited in four places is usually doing four jobs badly.

    They stay hidden for an honest reason, not a lazy one. Each felt obvious the moment you made it, so writing it down felt like recording that water is wet. The cost lands later, once the obviousness has evaporated.

    Before. In March you call the treatment "the road" everywhere. In July a co-author points out that "market access" is the accurate concept, since a road ending 30 km short of a market does little. You fix the abstract and the introduction, the files open that afternoon; the data section still says "road", Figure 2's axis reads "road completion year", the appendix says "connection". In September a balance table becomes Table 2, so heterogeneity slides from 3 to 4: your text names it three times, you fix two, miss the one in the conclusion, and your script still writes tab3_het.tex.

    After. Both decisions were written down as they were made. One line fixes the name to road access and bans "the road", "market access" and "connection", so a single plain-text search returns 31 hits in six files and you fix 31, prose and axis labels and captions together. A second line records that the heterogeneity table shows effects by distance to market, comes from 04_het.R, and is called tab_het in every note, so inserting a table changes one line and no cross-references. Same research, both times.

    Why writing them down pays even alone¶

    The rule: the person who most needs your decisions written down is a version of you who has forgotten them. The objection runs the other way, that this is overhead for teams and you are one person who remembers your own project. I made it for years, and it is wrong for exactly that reason.

    Future-you at the next seminar. Remember the headline from the top of the page: 0.14, standard error 0.05. Work those two numbers through. A 95% confidence interval is roughly the coefficient plus or minus two standard errors, so 0.14 − 0.10 = 0.04 at the bottom and 0.14 + 0.10 = 0.24 at the top. The t-statistic is the coefficient over its standard error, 0.14 / 0.05 = 2.8, past the 1.96 that convention treats as the 5% threshold. You know the effect is positive; you don't know how close to zero it sits.

    That 0.05 is the district-clustered standard error, and why district rather than village, given a road built village by village, is what the discussant asked: roads were approved district by district, so villages inside a district aren't independent draws. Cluster at village level and it falls to 0.032, lifting the t-statistic to 0.14 / 0.032 = 4.4. Both clear 1.96, so the finding survives either way. The word "precisely" doesn't: the honest interval still runs 0.04 to 0.24, a factor of six end to end.

    Economists differ on how conservative to be about clustering, and my point isn't which camp wins. It's that you worked the reason out over a day last spring and lost it. One line written that day answers the discussant in the room; without it you re-derive, lose two days, and land on a sample rule that is defensible but not quite the one behind the draft's numbers, so every table gets re-run.

    Future-you when the referee letter finally comes. The same question comes back years later, from Referee 2, in writing, with the paper's fate attached: in economics a first submission typically takes two to three years to reach a revise-and-resubmit. The clustering question is not the only one waiting. "Why drop 2015?" has a good answer: the census changed its crop categories, so the outcome isn't comparable across the break. It also has a bad answer, which is any answer composed after the question arrives, because by then you can't tell yourself apart from someone rationalising. That is the analysis phase's own test from the arc, arriving years late instead of in the spring when it cost nothing.

    Write the reason down the day you make the choice, not the day you're asked for it. Where it goes matters less than that it goes somewhere; the three we keep are a terminology file, a list of exhibits, and a short plan per section carrying each paragraph's lead. Two of those I'd argue for hard, terminology and the exhibit list, because I've watched both go wrong on every multi-author paper I've been part of. Leads I hold loosely: on a short paper you can carry them in your head, and some people genuinely draft better without them.

    When this stops being optional¶

    Everything above holds with no AI near the project; it held for the generation who wrote their papers on a typewriter. What an AI collaborator changes is the price of leaving a decision unwritten. A co-author who doesn't know you renamed the treatment will probably ask. An assistant that cannot see the decision will not ask: it writes "market access" into your data section, fast and confidently, across eight files while you make coffee. So the decisions have to live somewhere an assistant reads before it writes. Which document holds each, and what stops it drifting, is Where AI fits the paper — loops, gates, and the store; terminology already has its own page.
