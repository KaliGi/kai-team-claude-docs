    Designing experiments¶

    Most experiments fail on the whiteboard, not in the data. A control that differs from treatment on more than the one thing you care about, a self-reported outcome where a real-stakes one was possible, a comprehension check that quietly deletes half your treatment group — these are decided in an afternoon and then baked in forever. The experiment-design skill is a thinking partner for that afternoon: it walks your design out of your head one question at a time, pressure-tests each choice against what the literature actually supports, and flags what you forgot.

    What it does¶

    Run it when you're planning primary-data collection with random assignment — a survey or information experiment, an online study on Prolific/MTurk. It's an interview, not a form: one question at a time, you react, it moves on. It has two modes:

    • design — build a new experiment from scratch (question → arms & cover story → outcomes → integrity checks → randomization → power → a written spec).
    • review — hand it a draft (your own, a co-author's, a student's) and it audits the design against the checklist and returns a graded findings list (HALT / HIGH / MEDIUM / LOW), each with a fix.

    It ends by producing a structured design spec that hands off to the pre-registration and irb-application skills (both shipped) — design here, then pre-register, then ethics.

    How it gives advice¶

    Two rules make the advice trustworthy rather than opinionated:

    1. Every choice is options with pros and cons, each with a citation — you decide. It never quietly picks the "sensible" thing. When a decision has real consequences (an active vs. a passive control, how to power a noisy outcome), it lays out the trade-offs with the source that warrants each, states which way it would lean and why, and waits. There is rarely one right answer; the right answer depends on the experiment.
    2. It reminds you what you forgot. Before it writes the spec, a completeness gate walks sixteen checkpoints — is there one incentivized primary outcome? is the control matched? a pilot plan? — and flags anything missing rather than letting a silent gap through.

    The load-bearing rules it enforces¶

    A handful of design rules catch most first-draft mistakes, so the skill holds them firm (each is grounded in the literature, and tuned to how careful applied work is actually done):

    • A matched (active) control, differing from treatment only in the payload of interest, so priming and attention difference out rather than confound the effect (Haaland, Roth & Wohlfart 2023).
    • One incentivized, real-stakes primary outcome (a donation, a real choice, an effort task) — self-reported attitudes are secondary.
    • Don't elicit the outcome's prior. Asking people their prior about the outcome primes and anchors it; the randomized control group already is your counterfactual. (Prior elicitation is for information-provision designs whose estimand is belief-updating — a special case, with its own guardrails.)
    • Never exclude on a post-treatment check. Dropping people who fail a comprehension or attention check measured after treatment breaks randomization and biases the effect (Montgomery, Nyhan & Torres 2018). Keep everyone (ITT); use the check as manipulation evidence or a pre-registered subsample/LATE.
    • Always pilot and pre-test before fielding, and calibrate the incentive size from the pilot — even for a design you've run before, if anything changed.
    • Fix the sample size in advance — no peeking-and-stopping (Simmons, Nelson & Simonsohn 2011); power for the effect you expect, and for noisy real-stakes outcomes consider pooling several experiments with a pre-specified pooled MDE.

    What it covers today (and what's on the roadmap)¶

    The deep, validated track is online survey / information experiments — the kind most common in applied micro and political economy. Field RCTs and lab experiments are genuinely different (clustering, take-up/compliance, spillovers; or session effects, repeated games, the strategy method) and are branched but not yet built — the skill will tell you plainly when your study is one of those and offer the shared design spine with that caveat, rather than pretending survey depth transfers. Those two tracks are on the roadmap.

    For the figures a design implies: a CONSORT participant-flow diagram and a design-timeline schematic route to the TikZ diagram skill; the balance table and treatment-effect plots route to /descriptives and /coef-plot (in the research house style — no significance stars, no colour-by-significance).

    Sources¶

    The skill's recommendations are grounded in, and cite, a standard toolkit — the full list with links is bundled in the skill's references/background-and-sources.md. The load-bearing ones:

    • Haaland, Roth & Wohlfart (2023), "Designing Information Provision Experiments," JEL 61(1):3–40 — matched controls, obfuscated follow-ups, power floors.
    • Stantcheva (2023), "How to Run Surveys," Annual Review of Economics 15:205–234.
    • Harrison & List (2004), "Field Experiments," JEL 42(4):1009–1055 — the lab/artefactual/framed/natural-field taxonomy.
    • Duflo, Glennerster & Kremer (2007), "Using Randomization… A Toolkit" — power, clustering, stratification.
    • List, Sadoff & Wagner (2011); Bruhn & McKenzie (2009); McKenzie (2012) — allocation, balance, and ANCOVA.
    • Montgomery, Nyhan & Torres (2018) — why post-treatment conditioning ruins an experiment; de Quidt, Haushofer & Roth (2018) — bounding experimenter demand; Lin (2013) — covariate adjustment that cannot hurt precision.
